# A1 — Core Disciplines Foundational Concepts

## The one-minute map

```text
1. Measure the athlete.
2. Explain the physiology.
3. Analyse movement.
4. Identify the main limiting factor.
5. Train and recover.
6. Protect long-term wellbeing.
```

The eight foundational disciplines are: **anthropometry, exercise physiology,
physiotherapy, biomechanics, biochemistry, strength and conditioning,
psychology, and nutrition**. Read them as one complete athlete-support team,
not as isolated subjects.

## High-yield facts

| Area | Remember |
| --- | --- |
| Anthropometry | **Body mass index (BMI) = mass (kg) / height² (m²).** It is a size index, not a direct body-fat measure; muscular athletes can be misclassified. Skinfolds, girths, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or other methods answer different body-composition questions. |
| Exercise physiology | The **adenosine triphosphate–phosphocreatine system (ATP–PCr)** gives the fastest power for very brief maximal work; glycolysis contributes strongly to high-intensity efforts; oxidative metabolism dominates sustained work. The systems overlap—do not memorise rigid “switch” times. |
| Physiotherapy | Rehabilitation is a staged exposure to load: protect the injured tissue, maintain capacity elsewhere, restore specific function, then expose the athlete to sport demands. |
| Biomechanics | **Force** changes linear motion; **torque = force × perpendicular moment arm** changes rotation. A longer moment arm increases torque for the same force. |
| Biochemistry | **Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)** is the immediate energy currency. Glycogen supports high-intensity work; protein provides amino acids for repair/adaptation but is not the primary rapid fuel for maximal sprinting. |
| Strength & conditioning | Apply **specificity, overload, progression, individualisation, variation and recovery**. Training adaptation is specific to the stimulus and dose. |
| Psychology | Arousal and performance often follow an inverted-U pattern, but the optimal zone is individual and task-dependent. Self-efficacy affects effort, persistence and response to setbacks. |
| Nutrition | Carbohydrate supports high-intensity training; protein supports repair/adaptation; fat is essential for energy and cell functions. Approximate energy values: carbohydrate 4 kcal/g, protein 4 kcal/g, fat 9 kcal/g. |

## Energy-system picture

| Energy system | Main strength | Typical exam example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adenosine triphosphate–phosphocreatine | Fastest energy supply, but small capacity | Very short maximal burst |
| Glycolytic | High energy-supply rate, with metabolite accumulation | A hard effort lasting longer than the first burst |
| Oxidative | Slower supply rate, but largest capacity | Sustained work and recovery between efforts |

All three systems work at the same time. The question is which one contributes most, not which one is switched on.

**Exam distinction:** “primary” does not mean “exclusive.” A 30-second effort uses all systems, with different relative contributions.

## Fast movement logic

1. Identify the task and external demand.
2. Identify the force, direction and lever arm.
3. Ask which tissue/system limits the task.
4. Change the training variable that targets that limiter.
5. Re-test the same meaningful outcome.

## Common traps

- BMI is not body-fat percentage.
- Lactate is not simply “the waste product”; it is a usable metabolic intermediate, while associated acidity and other factors contribute to fatigue.
- Flexibility, mobility, stability, strength and power are not interchangeable.
- More training is not automatically better: adaptation requires an appropriate **stimulus–recovery balance**.
- Correlation between a biomarker and performance does not prove that changing the biomarker will improve performance.

## Easy ways to remember it

- Say the eight disciplines aloud in this order: **anthropometry, exercise physiology, physiotherapy, biomechanics, biochemistry, strength and conditioning, psychology, nutrition**.
- In an applied question, ask three plain questions: **What force is involved? Which tissue or body system receives it? What is limiting the athlete?**
- For food questions, remember the simple rule: **carbohydrate mainly fuels hard work, protein supports repair, and fat supports energy and body functions.**

## Self-test

1. Why can a rugby forward have a high BMI without high adiposity?
2. Which system supplies the fastest ATP resynthesis, and why is it not the only system used?
3. If the same force is applied farther from a joint centre, what happens to torque?
4. Why should a rehabilitation exercise eventually resemble the athlete’s sport task?
