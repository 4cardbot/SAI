# A1 — Core Disciplines Foundational Concepts

## The one-minute map

```text
1. Measure the athlete.
2. Explain the physiology.
3. Analyse movement.
4. Identify the main limiting factor.
5. Train and recover.
6. Protect long-term wellbeing.
```

The eight foundational disciplines are: **anthropometry, exercise physiology,
physiotherapy, biomechanics, biochemistry, strength and conditioning,
psychology, and nutrition**. Read them as one complete athlete-support team,
not as isolated subjects.

## High-yield facts

| Area | Remember |
| --- | --- |
| Anthropometry | **Body mass index (BMI) = mass (kg) / height² (m²).** It is a size index, not a direct body-fat measure; muscular athletes can be misclassified. Skinfolds, girths, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or other methods answer different body-composition questions. |
| Exercise physiology | The **adenosine triphosphate–phosphocreatine system (ATP–PCr)** gives the fastest power for very brief maximal work; glycolysis contributes strongly to high-intensity efforts; oxidative metabolism dominates sustained work. The systems overlap—do not memorise rigid “switch” times. |
| Physiotherapy | Rehabilitation is a staged exposure to load: protect the injured tissue, maintain capacity elsewhere, restore specific function, then expose the athlete to sport demands. |
| Biomechanics | **Force** changes linear motion; **torque = force × perpendicular moment arm** changes rotation. A longer moment arm increases torque for the same force. |
| Biochemistry | **Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)** is the immediate energy currency. Glycogen supports high-intensity work; protein provides amino acids for repair/adaptation but is not the primary rapid fuel for maximal sprinting. |
| Strength & conditioning | Apply **specificity, overload, progression, individualisation, variation and recovery**. Training adaptation is specific to the stimulus and dose. |
| Psychology | Arousal and performance often follow an inverted-U pattern, but the optimal zone is individual and task-dependent. Self-efficacy affects effort, persistence and response to setbacks. |
| Nutrition | Carbohydrate supports high-intensity training; protein supports repair/adaptation; fat is essential for energy and cell functions. Approximate energy values: carbohydrate 4 kcal/g, protein 4 kcal/g, fat 9 kcal/g. |

## Energy-system picture

| Energy system | Main strength | Typical exam example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adenosine triphosphate–phosphocreatine | Fastest energy supply, but small capacity | Very short maximal burst |
| Glycolytic | High energy-supply rate, with metabolite accumulation | A hard effort lasting longer than the first burst |
| Oxidative | Slower supply rate, but largest capacity | Sustained work and recovery between efforts |

All three systems work at the same time. The question is which one contributes most, not which one is switched on.

**Exam distinction:** “primary” does not mean “exclusive.” A 30-second effort uses all systems, with different relative contributions.

## Fast movement logic

1. Identify the task and external demand.
2. Identify the force, direction and lever arm.
3. Ask which tissue/system limits the task.
4. Change the training variable that targets that limiter.
5. Re-test the same meaningful outcome.

## Common traps

- BMI is not body-fat percentage.
- Lactate is not simply “the waste product”; it is a usable metabolic intermediate, while associated acidity and other factors contribute to fatigue.
- Flexibility, mobility, stability, strength and power are not interchangeable.
- More training is not automatically better: adaptation requires an appropriate **stimulus–recovery balance**.
- Correlation between a biomarker and performance does not prove that changing the biomarker will improve performance.

## Easy ways to remember it

- Say the eight disciplines aloud in this order: **anthropometry, exercise physiology, physiotherapy, biomechanics, biochemistry, strength and conditioning, psychology, nutrition**.
- In an applied question, ask three plain questions: **What force is involved? Which tissue or body system receives it? What is limiting the athlete?**
- For food questions, remember the simple rule: **carbohydrate mainly fuels hard work, protein supports repair, and fat supports energy and body functions.**

## Self-test

1. Why can a rugby forward have a high BMI without high adiposity?
2. Which system supplies the fastest ATP resynthesis, and why is it not the only system used?
3. If the same force is applied farther from a joint centre, what happens to torque?
4. Why should a rehabilitation exercise eventually resemble the athlete’s sport task?

# A1 — Testing Parameters, Standardization & Athlete Assessment

## Measurement vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Memory hook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Validity | Measures what it claims to measure | **V = target** |
| Reliability | Repeats consistently under similar conditions | **R = repeat** |
| Objectivity | Little dependence on who administers/scored it | **O = observer-proof** |
| Sensitivity | True-positive rate; detects people who have the condition | **Sensitive = catches** |
| Specificity | True-negative rate; excludes people who do not have the condition | **Specific = clears** |
| Responsiveness | Detects meaningful change over time | **Response = change** |
| Norm-referenced | Compared with a reference population | “How do you compare?” |
| Criterion-referenced | Compared with a meaningful standard/threshold | “Did you meet the target?” |

## The formulas worth writing once

In these formulas, **TP** means true positive, **TN** true negative, **FP**
false positive, and **FN** false negative. **PPV** means positive predictive
value and **NPV** negative predictive value. **SD** means standard deviation;
**ICC** means intraclass correlation coefficient; **SEM** means standard error
of measurement; **MDC** means the smallest change likely to be real; and
**LSI** means limb symmetry index. **CV** means coefficient of variation;
**z** means standard score; and **MDC95** means the minimum detectable change
at 95% confidence.

```text
Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)        Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
PPV = TP / (TP + FP)                 NPV = TN / (TN + FN)
CV% = (SD / mean) × 100              z = (individual − reference mean) / reference SD
SEM = SD × √(1 − ICC)                MDC95 = 1.96 × √2 × SEM
LSI% = involved side / uninvolved side × 100
```

**Interpretation:** a negative result from a very sensitive test is useful for
ruling out a condition; a positive result from a very specific test is useful
for ruling it in. Predictive values change with prevalence.

## Standardisation sequence

Use this sequence for a safe, repeatable test:

1. State the purpose of the test.
2. Screen safety and obtain informed consent.
3. Familiarise the athlete with the task.
4. Calibrate the equipment.
5. Standardise preparation and the testing environment.
6. Administer the test and record the raw data.
7. Check data quality and compare it with the correct reference.
8. Make the training decision and schedule a repeat test.

### Control before the athlete arrives

- Same time of day where practical; record sleep, food, caffeine, hydration, medication, recent training and illness.
- Same warm-up, instructions, equipment, surface, footwear, tester and rest intervals.
- Calibrate instruments and record serial number/software version when relevant.
- Use the same order of tests; fatigue can contaminate later results.
- Define stopping criteria, emergency plan and contraindications before testing.
- Obtain informed consent; explain purpose, risks, expected discomfort, data use and the right to stop.

### Choose a battery by question, not by habit

Choose a test in five steps: **identify the practical question, decide what
ability or quality must be measured, choose a valid test, use a reliable
protocol, and make sure the result can change a decision.**

Example: “Why is repeated sprint output falling?” may require sprint time, recovery heart rate, **rating of perceived exertion (RPE)**, sleep/travel and training-load context—not a single laboratory value.

## Lab versus field

| Laboratory | Field |
| --- | --- |
| More control and often greater measurement precision | More sport-specific and ecologically valid |
| Costly, less accessible, sometimes less representative | More affected by weather, surface and tester variation |
| Useful for mechanism and precise comparison | Useful for repeated monitoring and decision-making |

## Common traps

- A reliable test can be reliably wrong; reliability does not guarantee validity.
- A statistically significant change smaller than the **minimum detectable change (MDC)** or the **minimum clinically important difference (MCID)** may not be actionable.
- “Gold standard” is not automatically the best field test for every athlete.
- Never compare scores collected under materially different protocols as if they were the same measurement.

## Easy ways to remember it

- **Validity asks “does it measure the right thing?” Reliability asks “would it repeat?”**
- A negative result from a very sensitive test is useful for ruling out a condition. A positive result from a very specific test is useful for ruling it in.
- Before every test, check five things in plain language: **Is the athlete safe? Is the athlete prepared? Is the method fixed? Is the equipment calibrated? Is the environment recorded?**

## Self-test

1. Which metric answers “how repeatable is this test?”
2. Why can a low-sensitivity negative test be falsely reassuring?
3. What is the practical difference between SEM and MDC?
4. Name five conditions that should remain constant in a longitudinal test.

# A1 — Emerging Technologies & Biostatistics

## Match the tool to the question

| Tool | Main output | High-yield limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global positioning or local positioning system (GPS/LPS) | Distance, speed, acceleration, high-speed running | Sampling rate, satellite/venue signal and device validity affect short bursts |
| Inertial measurement unit (IMU) | Acceleration, angular velocity, orientation, impacts | Placement, drift and algorithm differences |
| Force plate | Ground-reaction force, impulse, rate of force development, asymmetry | Expensive; a single jump is not the whole athlete |
| Motion capture/video | Joint angles, timing, technique | Marker/occlusion or camera-angle error; 2-D can hide 3-D motion |
| Heart rate and heart-rate variability (HR/HRV) | Cardiovascular response/autonomic proxy | Strongly affected by sleep, illness, posture, breathing and measurement conditions |
| Electromyography (EMG) | Electrical muscle activity timing/amplitude proxy | Crosstalk, electrode placement and normalisation matter |
| Athlete-management platform | Merges wellness, load, injury and availability data | Garbage in; privacy/access control; dashboards can create false certainty |
| Artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) | Classification, prediction, pattern detection | Needs representative data, external validation and explainability |

## Descriptive statistics

```text
Mean = Σx / n                         Range = maximum − minimum
Sample variance = Σ(x − mean)²/(n−1)  Standard deviation (SD) = √variance
Coefficient of variation (CV%) = SD/mean × 100
Standard score (z) = (x − reference mean) / reference SD
```

- **Mean:** sensitive to outliers; useful for roughly symmetric data.
- **Median:** robust to skew/outliers; report the interquartile range (IQR) with it.
- **Standard deviation:** spread around the mean; not the same as standard error.
- **Confidence interval (CI):** plausible range for a population estimate, not the probability that a fixed parameter is inside the interval.

## Inferential/statistical literacy

| Concept | What it actually says |
| --- | --- |
| p-value | How unusual the data would be under the null model; not the probability the hypothesis is true |
| 95% confidence interval (CI) | Precision/uncertainty around an estimate; check whether it crosses a meaningful threshold |
| Effect size | Magnitude of a difference/relationship; pair with uncertainty and context |
| Type I error | False positive; reject a true null |
| Type II error | False negative; miss a real effect |
| Correlation | Association, not causation |
| Practical significance | Whether the change matters to the athlete, not merely whether p < 0.05 |

## Choosing a visual

| If you want to show… | Use… |
| --- | --- |
| Change over time | A line chart |
| Differences between groups | A dot plot or box plot |
| How values are distributed | A histogram |
| The relationship between two variables | A scatter plot |
| Parts that make up a whole | A stacked bar chart |
| Agreement between two measurement methods | A Bland–Altman plot |

## Data-quality checklist

For data quality, follow this checklist: **define the variable, check the
units, inspect missing values and unusual values, verify the sensor and test
protocol, decide the analysis before looking for a preferred result, show the
uncertainty, explain the practical decision, and protect privacy.**

**Do not confuse:** accuracy (closeness to truth), precision (repeatability), validity (construct/criterion meaning), and reliability (consistency).

## Easy ways to remember it

- Match the tool to its job: **positioning systems track travel, inertial units track movement, force plates measure pushes, cameras show angles, and electromyography shows muscle electrical activity.**
- For statistics, remember three questions: **Where is the centre? How spread out are the values? How uncertain is the estimate?**
- When you see “statistically significant,” immediately ask: **How large is the change? How certain is it? Does it matter to this athlete?**

## Self-test

1. Which sensor is most directly suited to measuring impulse?
2. When would median/IQR be preferable to mean/SD?
3. Why is a model with excellent training accuracy not automatically useful in a new team?
4. What is the difference between statistical and practical significance?

# A1 — Data Interpretation & Applied Analytics

## The performance-analyst loop

Use this decision cycle:

1. Ask a clear performance question.
2. Establish a baseline.
3. Collect comparable data.
4. Check and clean the data.
5. Display the pattern.
6. Interpret it with uncertainty.
7. Communicate the finding and intervention.
8. Re-test to see whether the decision helped.

The output is not a dashboard; it is a **defensible decision**.

## Useful calculations

```text
Absolute change = post − pre
% change = (post − pre) / pre × 100
Training monotony = weekly mean daily load / standard deviation of daily load
Training strain = weekly load × monotony
Session load = session duration (min) × rating of perceived exertion
Limb symmetry index (LSI) = involved / uninvolved × 100
```

Use acute:chronic workload ratios cautiously. They are summaries of load history, not a universal injury threshold or a substitute for athlete context.

## Read a performance profile

For each athlete, record four things in a performance profile:

| Area | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| Strengths | What can the athlete already do well? |
| Limiters | Which important, changeable weakness is restricting performance? |
| Recovery and health | Is the athlete available and recovering sufficiently? |
| Speed and sport demands | Which qualities must transfer to the actual sport task? |

For each domain record: **current level, target, confidence in measurement, trend, limiting factor, action and review date**. A low score is not automatically the limiter; a high-impact, modifiable deficit is more actionable.

## Longitudinal interpretation

- Compare like with like: same test, protocol, timing, environment and athlete state.
- Plot individual data, not only team averages; averages can hide responders and non-responders.
- Separate **signal** from **noise** using reliability, measurement error, the minimum detectable change and repeated observations.
- Mark illness, travel, menstrual-cycle context where voluntarily and appropriately shared, injury and major training changes on the timeline.
- Use thresholds as prompts for review, not as automatic diagnoses.

## Analytics decisions

| Pattern | First question | Sensible action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Performance down, wellness normal | Test error? fatigue from unrecorded load? | Recheck protocol and training exposure |
| Performance down, wellness down | Recovery/illness/travel? | Reduce or modify load; screen clinically |
| One sensor abnormal | Device/placement/algorithm? | Cross-check with another measure |
| Group average stable, athlete variable | Who is hidden by the mean? | Individualise monitoring |
| Strong correlation | Is the relationship causal and useful? | Examine timing, confounding and intervention evidence |

## Communication template

Report every finding in this order: **what changed, how confident you are,
why it matters to performance, what you recommend, and when you will review it.**

Example: “Repeated-sprint time is 4% slower than the athlete’s reliable baseline across two sessions; sleep and recent high-speed volume are also reduced. Treat this as a recovery flag, modify today’s high-speed exposure, and reassess after recovery.”

## Easy ways to remember it

- For every result, work through five plain questions: **What is the context? Am I comparing like with like? Could this be measurement error? What action follows? When will I check again?**
- Every chart must answer: **What changed? Compared with what? How sure are we? What will we do?**

# A1 — Performance Enhancement & Recovery Protocols

## Programme logic

Build a programme in this order: **set the goal, analyse the sport demand,
measure a baseline, prescribe the training dose, plan recovery, monitor the
response, adjust the dose, and expose the athlete to the sport task.**

Set the dose using **frequency, intensity, time and type**. Add volume, density, complexity and velocity when appropriate.

## Training principles

| Principle | Exam meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Specificity | Adaptation follows the energy system, movement, velocity and decision demand trained |
| Overload | Stimulus exceeds the athlete’s current habitual demand |
| Progression | Increase challenge gradually and deliberately |
| Individualisation | Dose depends on athlete, sport, position, history and readiness |
| Variation/periodisation | Organise stress and recovery across time; avoid random change |
| Reversibility | Unused adaptations decline with detraining |
| Recovery | Adaptation is built between exposures, not during unbroken stress |

## Strength versus power

```text
Strength = force capacity
Power = force × velocity
```

An athlete can be strong but slow. To improve power, preserve force while training intent and movement velocity; select loads and exercises that fit the task.

## Recovery hierarchy

```text
1. Fix the dose: training, competition and travel load
2. Sleep and circadian routine
3. Fuel and hydration
4. Active recovery / mobility / psychological down-regulation
5. Targeted modalities when a clear purpose and tolerable evidence exist
```

| Tool | Use carefully |
| --- | --- |
| Active recovery | Low-intensity movement may reduce perceived stiffness; it is not a cure for excessive load |
| Cryotherapy/heat | Context-specific effects; do not use a modality to mask danger or replace diagnosis |
| Compression | May help symptoms/recovery perception in some settings; individual response varies |
| Massage | Short-term symptoms/relaxation; does not replace progressive loading |
| Sleep | Protect duration, regularity, dark/cool room and pre-sleep routine; individual need varies |

## Injury-risk lens

The risk or readiness decision should consider five pieces of information:
**the work completed, the athlete’s internal response, tissue capacity,
recovery, and context such as illness, travel or heat.**

Progress load by response and criteria, not calendar alone. Watch spikes, monotonous training, sudden high-speed exposure, illness, low energy availability and poor sleep.

## Nutrition and supplements

- Carbohydrate: high-intensity training and glycogen restoration.
- Protein: distribute adequate high-quality protein across the day; increase attention during immobilisation/rehabilitation.
- Hydration: individualise to sweat rate, climate, duration and sodium losses; avoid both large dehydration and overdrinking.
- For supplements, check these in order: **food first, evidence, contamination risk, athlete-specific need and third-party testing**. No supplement is automatically safe because it is “natural.” The **World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA)** rules still apply.

## Easy ways to remember it

- A good programme answers four questions: **Is it specific? Is it difficult enough? Is it progressed gradually? Is recovery planned?**
- Put recovery in this order: **correct the training dose, protect sleep, provide food and fluids, then consider extra recovery tools.**
- Power requires both **force and speed**. A plan that trains only force is incomplete when the sport requires fast movement.

# A1 — Holistic Development, Travel & Ethics

## The athlete is a system

An athlete’s availability and long-term performance depend on several areas:

| Area | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Physical capacity | Determines what the athlete can do |
| Mental health | Affects motivation, confidence, concentration and recovery |
| Fuel and energy | Supports training, healing and normal body functions |
| Sleep and travel | Affect alertness, adaptation and readiness |
| Relationships and ethics | Affect safety, trust and sustainable participation |

Use a **biopsychosocial** lens: a performance change may be physical, psychological, social, environmental—or several at once.

## High-yield wellbeing concepts

| Term | Distinguish it from |
| --- | --- |
| Overreaching | Short-term planned/unplanned performance dip; may be functional when followed by recovery |
| Overtraining syndrome | Persistent maladaptation with prolonged performance decline and broader symptoms; diagnosis is not made from one bad session |
| Burnout | Exhaustion, reduced accomplishment and sport devaluation; not simply ordinary fatigue |
| Relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S) | Health/performance consequences of low energy availability affecting multiple physiological systems; can affect any sex |
| Long-term athlete development (LTAD) | Development is long-term and stage-sensitive; avoid adult training assumptions for growing athletes |

## Travel and environmental readiness

| Travel stage | Main actions |
| --- | --- |
| Before departure | Plan time-zone adjustment, sleep, food, fluids, medicines and emergency contacts |
| During travel | Move regularly, hydrate, protect sleep, manage meals and reduce infection exposure |
| After arrival | Set a local sleep/light routine, monitor symptoms and reintroduce hard training gradually |

- **Eastward travel** usually requires advancing the body clock; **westward** travel usually requires delaying it. The direction of light exposure matters; do not give blanket “bright light at any time” advice.
- Shift gradually where possible; use a consistent sleep/wake anchor, morning/evening light strategically, and avoid alcohol or unprescribed sedatives.
- At altitude, hypobaric hypoxia increases ventilatory and cardiovascular stress. Headache, nausea, dizziness and sleep disturbance require monitoring; worsening neurological or respiratory symptoms are red flags.
- In heat/humidity, acclimatisation, work–rest planning, cooling and individual hydration strategy matter. Suspected exertional heat stroke is an emergency, not a “push through” problem.

## Ethics checklist

Ethical practice means **obtaining consent, working within competence, keeping
information confidential, managing conflicts of interest, supporting clean
sport, safeguarding the athlete, keeping accurate records, and escalating a
serious concern.**

- Share only necessary health data with the appropriate team member.
- Explain risks, benefits, alternatives and data use; consent is voluntary and can be withdrawn.
- Record facts, not speculation; correct errors transparently.
- Disclose conflicts of interest and do not let selection pressure override welfare.
- The athlete-support team educates about anti-doping and **therapeutic use exemptions (TUEs)**; it does not prescribe shortcuts.

## Easy ways to remember travel and ethics

Before travel, prepare a **sleep plan, food and fluids, emergency contacts, medicines and consent for information sharing**. During travel, protect sleep, move regularly and record important changes. After arrival, adjust light and sleep gradually, then reintroduce hard training only when the athlete is ready.

For ethical questions, choose the response that protects **safety, autonomy, privacy, accurate records and fair treatment**.

# A2 — Anti-Doping Regulations & Clean Sport

## The clean-sport chain

Clean sport has four parts:

1. Know the current Prohibited List and the World Anti-Doping Code.
2. Check medicines and supplements, and apply for a therapeutic use exemption when the criteria are met.
3. Follow testing, whereabouts and sample-integrity requirements.
4. Understand results management, hearings and appeals.

The **2026 World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) Prohibited List took effect on 1 January 2026**. Always distinguish the current List from the broader Code and from Indian implementation through the **National Anti-Doping Agency India (NADA India)**.

## The 11 anti-doping rule violations

| Article | Cue |
| --- | --- |
| 2.1 | Presence of a prohibited substance/metabolite/marker in a sample |
| 2.2 | Use or attempted use |
| 2.3 | Evading, refusing or failing to submit to sample collection |
| 2.4 | Whereabouts failure by a whereabouts athlete |
| 2.5 | Tampering or attempted tampering |
| 2.6 | Possession |
| 2.7 | Trafficking or attempted trafficking |
| 2.8 | Administration or attempted administration |
| 2.9 | Complicity or attempted complicity |
| 2.10 | Prohibited association |
| 2.11 | Discouraging/reporting retaliation or other prohibited retaliation |

To remember the violations, group them instead of memorising a long letter string: **what was found in the sample; what the athlete or support person did; possession, trafficking or association; and failures in the testing process.**

## Rules that appear in stems

- **Strict liability:** for presence, the athlete is responsible for what is found in the sample; intent, fault or negligence is not required to establish the basic presence violation. Sanction/defence questions are separate.
- **Athlete responsibility:** check medicines and supplements, disclose relevant use, follow testing procedures, keep whereabouts where applicable and ask before acting when unsure.
- **Support-person responsibility:** do not administer, traffic, conceal, pressure, tamper or retaliate.
- **Supplement risk:** “natural,” over-the-counter or trusted by a teammate does not mean contamination-free.

## Therapeutic use exemption (TUE): four-part decision test

For a therapeutic use exemption, ask four questions:

1. Does the athlete have a genuine medical condition?
2. Is the treatment needed to restore normal health?
3. Is there no reasonable permitted alternative?
4. Will the treatment not improve performance beyond normal health?

Emergency/retroactive therapeutic use exemption rules are narrow and documentation-dependent. A TUE is not a general permission slip; verify the current National Anti-Doping Agency India/World Anti-Doping Agency process before treatment.

## Prohibited-list categories

- **S0** non-approved substances.
- **S1** anabolic agents; **S2** peptide hormones/growth factors; **S3** beta-2 agonists; **S4** hormone/metabolic modulators; **S5** diuretics/masking agents.
- **S6** stimulants; **S7** narcotics; **S8** cannabinoids; **S9** glucocorticoids.
- **M1–M3** prohibited methods; **P1** beta-blockers in particular sports.
- “In-competition only” is not the same as “always prohibited”; check route, dose, sport and formulation.

## Easy ways to remember it

- For any medicine or supplement, do three things: **check the current list, check the route and competition status, and look up the exact product.**
- Keep a written record of the **medical reason, source check, exemption decision if needed and final treatment decision**.
- For a question, first decide whether it is about **what was found in a sample, what someone did, possession or association, or the testing process**.

# A2 — Sports Governance, Ethics & Safeguarding

## Safe-sport response tree

If you observe or receive a safeguarding concern:

1. If there is immediate danger, protect the athlete and call for emergency help.
2. Listen calmly without leading the athlete or promising absolute secrecy.
3. Record factual observations and preserve relevant evidence.
4. Report through the organisation’s designated safeguarding channel.
5. Support the athlete, prevent retaliation and document follow-up.

Do not investigate alone, confront the alleged person in a way that increases risk, or circulate details on a team chat.

## Governance map

| Layer | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Olympic Charter / International Olympic Committee (IOC) | What principles govern the Olympic Movement? |
| Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports (MYAS) / national framework | What national policy and recognition framework applies? |
| Sports Authority of India (SAI) | How are athlete-development and support programmes implemented? |
| Indian Olympic Association (IOA) | How is India represented in the Olympic Movement? |
| National Sports Federation (NSF) | Who administers a particular sport, its selection and technical pathway? |
| Ethics/safeguarding bodies | How are welfare, conflicts, complaints and safe participation protected? |

## National Sports Governance Act: exam caution

The **National Sports Governance Act, 2025** creates a statutory framework for national sports bodies, ethics, athlete representation, dispute resolution and accountable governance. The commencement clause matters: an Act can be enacted but provisions may require a government commencement notification. For current-status MCQs, use the official notification rather than assuming every provision is already operational.

## Ethics flash list

- **Autonomy:** respect the athlete’s informed choice.
- **Beneficence/non-maleficence:** promote welfare; avoid preventable harm.
- **Justice:** fair access and non-discrimination.
- **Confidentiality:** share minimum necessary information with legitimate recipients.
- **Competence:** work within scope; refer when required.
- **Conflict of interest:** disclose and manage competing incentives.
- **Integrity:** accurate records, honest reporting, no manipulation for selection.

## Safeguarding vocabulary

Safeguarding covers prevention and response to physical, sexual and emotional abuse, neglect, bullying, harassment, discrimination, exploitation and retaliation. A professional’s job is not only to recognise a visible injury; abrupt behaviour change, unexplained injuries, fear of a staff member, secrecy or coercive control can be warning signs.

## Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (POSH) exam cues

Under India’s POSH framework, remember: an aggrieved woman can complain about sexual harassment at a workplace; the Internal Committee handles complaints where applicable; confidentiality protects the complaint/inquiry; employers have prevention and redressal duties. Time limits and procedure should be read from the current Act/policy when a question asks for an exact number.

## Easy ways to remember it

- In an athlete-welfare concern, **listen, report through the correct channel, record facts and support the athlete**.
- In an ethics question, choose the response that protects **consent, autonomy, respect and fair treatment**.
- For governance, think from broad to local: **the Olympic Charter, the Ministry, the national sports organisations, the team policy and finally the athlete.**

# B — Biomechanics & Movement Analysis

## Kinematics versus kinetics

```text
Kinematics: what moves?      position, displacement, velocity, acceleration, angle
Kinetics: why does it move?  force, torque, impulse, work, power, momentum
```

## Formula strip

```text
v = displacement / time                 a = Δv / Δt
F = m × a                               torque (τ) = force × perpendicular moment arm
p = m × v                               impulse = force × time = change in momentum
work = force × displacement              power = work / time = force × velocity
pressure = force / area                  mechanical advantage = output force / input force
```

Vector questions require **magnitude and direction**. Ground-reaction force is the external force from the ground; it is not automatically the same as muscular force.

## Levers

| Class | Order | Common body example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | F–axis–R | Head/neck extension |
| Second | axis–R–F | Heel raise |
| Third | axis–F–R | Biceps flexing the elbow |

Most human limbs favour speed and range of motion over force advantage. Increasing a moment arm increases torque for the same muscle force, but changes movement speed and joint loading.

## Stability and alignment

```text
Stability increases when: the centre of mass stays over the base of support,
the base is wider, the centre of mass is lower, friction is adequate,
and the external disturbance is manageable.
```

Dynamic alignment questions often use **hip–knee–foot**: poor hip control can contribute to dynamic knee valgus, but the cause must be tested rather than assumed.

## High-yield joint mechanics

- **Scapulohumeral rhythm:** a commonly taught overall 2:1 glenohumeral-to-scapulothoracic contribution during full elevation; actual ratios vary by range, task and person.
- **Knee screw-home:** terminal extension couples tibial external rotation relative to femur in open chain (or femoral internal rotation relative to tibia in closed chain); popliteus “unlocks” the knee.
- **Windlass mechanism:** toe dorsiflexion tensions plantar fascia, raises the medial arch and links foot stiffness to propulsion.
- **Subtalar pronation/supination:** triplanar motions; avoid reducing them to one plane.

## Walking sequence

| Part of walking | Sub-phases in order |
| --- | --- |
| Stance | Initial contact, loading response, mid-stance, terminal stance, pre-swing |
| Swing | Initial swing, mid-swing, terminal swing |

Initial swing produces much of peak knee flexion for foot clearance. Terminal swing prepares limb positioning for the next initial contact. Interpret gait by timing, joint motion, moments, muscle action and task speed together.

## Sport movement cues

- Jump landing: absorb force through coordinated hip/knee/ankle flexion; trunk position, knee alignment, stiffness and landing strategy interact.
- Throwing phases are **wind-up, cocking, acceleration, deceleration and follow-through**; deceleration is a major load-management issue.
- Sprinting: acceleration and maximal velocity differ in trunk angle, ground-contact time, step length/frequency and horizontal force orientation.

## Easy ways to remember it

- **Kinematics describes the movement; kinetics explains the forces that cause or control it.**
- For levers, identify the middle item: first-class has the joint axis in the middle, second-class has the resistance in the middle, and third-class has the effort in the middle.
- Learn the walking sequence as words: **initial contact, loading response, mid-stance, terminal stance, pre-swing, initial swing, mid-swing, terminal swing.**

# B — Human Physiology & Exercise Physiology

## Energy systems

| System | Rate/capacity | Typical use cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adenosine triphosphate–phosphocreatine (ATP–PCr) | Highest rate, low capacity | Immediate maximal efforts |
| Anaerobic glycolysis | High rate, moderate capacity | Hard efforts lasting longer than the immediate burst; metabolites accumulate |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | Lower rate, high capacity | Sustained work and recovery between efforts |

All three operate together. “Aerobic versus anaerobic” is a relative-contribution question, not an on/off switch.

## Muscle physiology

Muscle contraction follows this sequence:

1. A nerve signal reaches the muscle.
2. Acetylcholine is released at the neuromuscular junction.
3. The muscle-fibre electrical signal travels along the fibre.
4. Calcium is released.
5. Calcium moves troponin so actin and myosin can form cross-bridges.
6. The cross-bridges cycle using adenosine triphosphate.

- **Henneman size principle:** smaller, lower-threshold motor units are recruited before larger, higher-threshold units as demand rises.
- Type I fibres: oxidative, fatigue-resistant, lower maximal force/power.
- Type II fibres: greater force/power and faster shortening, generally more fatigable; training and task alter phenotype and function.
- Isometric = tension without visible joint movement; concentric = muscle shortens; eccentric = muscle lengthens under tension.

## Cardiovascular equations

```text
Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume
Oxygen uptake = cardiac output × (arterial oxygen − venous oxygen difference)  [Fick principle]
Mean arterial pressure ≈ diastolic pressure + 1/3(systolic pressure − diastolic pressure)
```

Acute exercise: heart rate, stroke volume (until high intensities in some conditions), cardiac output, ventilation and systolic blood pressure generally rise; blood flow is redistributed according to demand and thermoregulation.

Training adaptations can include lower submaximal heart rate, increased stroke volume, improved oxygen extraction, plasma-volume change and improved economy—depending on programme and athlete.

## Threshold and fatigue

- Lactate threshold/ventilatory threshold marks a change in the relationship between work and metabolic/ventilatory response; exact definitions vary by test.
- Fatigue is multifactorial: substrate availability, metabolite/ion changes, neuromuscular drive, thermoregulation, central factors, pain and motivation may contribute.
- A blood biomarker alone rarely explains performance.

## Thermoregulation and altitude

In heat, the body sends more blood to the skin and produces sweat; evaporation
of that sweat removes heat. High humidity reduces evaporation and therefore
increases heat strain. At altitude, lower oxygen pressure increases breathing
and cardiovascular stress.

Heat acclimatisation is progressive exposure with monitoring. Altitude illness is clinical; headache plus nausea, dizziness, sleep disturbance or deteriorating neurological/respiratory symptoms should not be treated as ordinary “fitness adaptation.”

## Recovery physiology

Recovery restores homeostasis and supports supercompensation, but the response depends on dose, sleep, food, illness, psychological stress and tissue injury. Do not use “more inflammation is always bad” or “more soreness means more adaptation” as rules.

## Easy ways to remember it

- Think of the energy systems as **fast, hard and long**: phosphocreatine is fastest, glycolysis supports hard efforts, and oxidative metabolism supports long efforts.
- Cardiac output means **how much blood the heart pumps each minute**, so it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Oxygen uptake equals cardiac output multiplied by the difference in oxygen content between arterial and venous blood.
- Muscle activation follows this order: **nerve signal, acetylcholine, calcium, troponin and finally the myosin cross-bridge.**

# B — Reproductive Physiology

This is a separate revision topic under the SAI syllabus area **Human Physiology: “Endocrine and reproductive systems.”** It is useful for understanding growth, puberty, menstrual function, energy availability and athlete health. It does not mean that every athlete should be judged by the same sex-based or cycle-based rule.

## The control pathway

```text
Hypothalamus
    ↓ releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone
Anterior pituitary gland
    ↓ releases follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone
Ovaries or testes
    ↓ release reproductive hormones and gametes
Target tissues and reproductive function
```

Most of this pathway uses **negative feedback**: when reproductive hormones rise, they usually reduce further stimulation from the brain and pituitary gland. A special exception occurs before ovulation, when sustained high estrogen changes to **positive feedback** and produces a sharp luteinizing-hormone surge.

## High-yield hormone map

| Hormone or structure | Main exam-relevant role |
| --- | --- |
| Follicle-stimulating hormone | Supports ovarian follicle development; in testes, acts mainly on Sertoli cells to support sperm production |
| Luteinizing hormone | Triggers ovulation; in testes, stimulates Leydig cells to produce testosterone |
| Estrogen | Supports female reproductive development and helps rebuild the uterine lining |
| Progesterone | Stabilises and prepares the uterine lining after ovulation and supports pregnancy |
| Testosterone | Supports male reproductive development, sperm production and anabolic tissues |
| Inhibin | Selectively reduces follicle-stimulating-hormone release when the gonads are producing gametes |
| Human chorionic gonadotropin | Early-pregnancy hormone that helps maintain progesterone production until the placenta takes over much of that role |

## Menstrual-cycle sequence

The cycle is variable; “28 days” is a teaching example, not a rule for every person.

```text
Menstruation → follicular phase → ovulation → luteal phase
     low hormones     estrogen rises       luteinizing-     progesterone rises
     lining sheds     follicle develops    hormone surge   corpus luteum forms
                                      ↓
                         no pregnancy: hormones fall → next menstruation
```

- **Menstruation:** the uterine lining is shed after estrogen and progesterone fall.
- **Follicular phase:** a follicle develops in the ovary and estrogen generally rises.
- **Ovulation:** the luteinizing-hormone surge releases the oocyte (egg cell).
- **Luteal phase:** the corpus luteum produces progesterone; if pregnancy does not occur, it regresses and hormone levels fall.

## Male reproductive physiology

- The **testes** produce sperm and testosterone.
- **Sertoli cells** support developing sperm; **Leydig cells** produce testosterone when stimulated by luteinizing hormone.
- Sperm production requires coordinated follicle-stimulating-hormone action, testosterone and suitable testicular temperature.

## Puberty, maturation and key terms

- **Puberty:** activation of the brain–pituitary–gonad pathway, producing reproductive maturity and secondary sexual characteristics.
- **Menarche:** the first menstrual bleeding.
- **Amenorrhea:** absence of menstruation. In an athlete, it should not automatically be dismissed as a normal training effect.
- **Ovulation:** release of an oocyte from an ovary.
- **Spermatogenesis:** production of sperm cells.
- **Gametogenesis:** production of reproductive cells—sperm or oocytes.

## Exercise and athlete health

- Training response varies between individuals. Do not prescribe a universal “best” training week solely from the menstrual-cycle phase; consider symptoms, performance, recovery, medical status and the athlete’s preferences.
- **Low energy availability** means that food energy left for normal body functions is inadequate after exercise expenditure is considered. It can disturb reproductive hormones and menstrual function, impair bone health and recovery, and contribute to **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport**.
- Repeated missed periods, cycle changes, reduced libido, persistent fatigue, stress injuries or unexplained performance decline deserve confidential assessment by the appropriate medical team. They are not proof of poor commitment or a reason to increase training automatically.
- In adolescents, biological maturation can change size, strength and reproductive function at different rates. Selection and training decisions should therefore use growth and health monitoring, not appearance alone.

## Memory tactics

- **“Brain → pituitary → gonad → target.”** Follow the pathway from the control centre to the final organ.
- **“Follicle-stimulating hormone supports; luteinizing hormone launches.”** The first supports follicles/sperm production; the second triggers ovulation or stimulates testosterone production.
- **“Estrogen builds; progesterone prepares.”** Estrogen generally rebuilds the uterine lining; progesterone makes it suitable for support after ovulation.
- For the cycle, remember **“shed, grow, release, support”**: menstruation, follicular growth, ovulation, luteal support.
- For an athlete with menstrual disturbance, ask **“fuel, load, health”**: is energy intake adequate, is training stress excessive, and is another medical cause possible?

# B — Electrotherapy & Electrodiagnosis

## Dose vocabulary

```text
Amplitude/intensity = how strong
Pulse duration/width = how long each pulse lasts
Frequency = pulses per second (hertz, Hz)
Duty cycle = on-time / total cycle time
Polarity = direction of current in relevant systems
```

Always pair modality with **indication, dose, contraindication, skin sensation, tissue stage and outcome measure**.

## Modality quick chart

| Modality | Exam anchor |
| --- | --- |
| Conventional transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) | Higher frequency, comfortable sensory intensity; gate-control framing is common |
| Low-frequency transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation | Lower frequency, stronger/motor-level tolerance; endogenous opioid mechanisms are commonly discussed |
| Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) | Evokes contraction to support activation/strength; electrode placement and voluntary effort matter |
| Interferential current (IFC) | Two medium-frequency currents intersect to create a beat/interference frequency; do not confuse carrier with beat frequency |
| Therapeutic ultrasound | 1 megahertz (MHz) is generally deeper; 3 megahertz is generally more superficial. Continuous = more thermal; pulsed = lower average thermal dose |
| Short-wave diathermy (SWD) | Radiofrequency heating; capacitive/inductive coupling and metal/implant safety are key |
| Cryotherapy | Reduces tissue temperature and can alter pain/nerve conduction; protect skin and circulation |
| Photobiomodulation | Dose, wavelength, energy density and target tissue matter; “laser” is not one uniform treatment |
| Extracorporeal shock-wave therapy (ESWT) | Radial and focused systems differ in physics and depth; use condition-specific evidence |

## Strength–duration concepts

```text
Rheobase = minimum current amplitude for a very long pulse
Chronaxie = pulse duration needed at twice rheobase to excite tissue
```

Denervated muscle requires different pulse characteristics from innervated muscle. Interpret a strength–duration curve with clinical examination and nerve status; do not diagnose from one curve alone.

## Safety screen

- Pacemaker/implanted electronic device: modality-specific caution/contraindication; follow device and local guidance.
- Avoid current/heat/light over active malignancy, uncontrolled bleeding, impaired sensation/circulation or areas where skin/tissue safety cannot be monitored.
- Consider pregnancy, open epiphyseal plates, infection, thrombosis, metal/implants and medication effects according to modality.
- Never use electrotherapy to postpone assessment of a red flag or to mask worsening neurological/vascular symptoms.

## Evidence-based choice

Choose a modality in five steps: **identify the clinical problem, define the
desired outcome, choose the best-supported option, select a safe dose, and
reassess whether a meaningful change occurred.**

The presence of a physiological effect does not prove a clinically meaningful outcome. For chronic nonspecific low-back pain, do not assume therapeutic ultrasound is essential merely because it produces tissue heating.

## Easy ways to remember it

- When choosing a current, check four settings in plain language: **how strong it is, how long each pulse lasts, how often pulses repeat, and how much of each cycle is switched on**.
- Therapeutic ultrasound: **1 megahertz is generally deeper and 3 megahertz is generally more superficial**; continuous treatment heats more than pulsed treatment.
- Electrodiagnosis: **rheobase is the current level; chronaxie is the time needed.**

# B — Exercise Therapy & Rehabilitation

## Rehabilitation staircase

## Rehabilitation staircase

Progress through these stages when the athlete meets the relevant criteria:

1. Protect the tissue and calm unnecessary aggravation.
2. Restore range of motion and muscle activation.
3. Build strength and general capacity.
4. Add balance, power, speed and change-of-direction work.
5. Practise the sport task.
6. Expose the athlete to unrestricted performance demands.

Progress by **response and criteria**, not by calendar alone.

## Exercise principles

**Specificity · overload · progression · individualisation · reversibility · variation · recovery.**

For every exercise specify: position, load, range, speed, sets/repetitions, rest, pain response, quality criterion and progression/regression.

## Contraction and loading

| Mode | Useful cue |
| --- | --- |
| Isometric | Tension at a fixed joint angle; useful when movement is irritable or for activation, but angle-specific |
| Concentric | Muscle shortens while producing force |
| Eccentric | Muscle lengthens under load; high force and tissue-loading stimulus; dose progressively |
| Isokinetic | Constant angular speed with accommodating resistance; specialised equipment |

For tendinopathy, progressive tendon loading may include isometrics, isotonic/eccentric or heavy-slow resistance and sport exposure; the best choice depends on stage, symptoms and capacity.

## Flexibility and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation

| Method | Main cue |
| --- | --- |
| Static | Held position; low ballistic demand |
| Dynamic | Controlled movement through range; useful in preparation |
| Ballistic | Bouncing/rapid end-range; higher demand and context-sensitive |
| Hold–relax | Isometric contraction followed by relaxation and increased range |
| Contract–relax | Concentric/isotonic contraction followed by relaxation and increased range |
| Agonist contraction | Antagonist relaxation facilitated by active agonist movement |

## Open versus closed chain

```text
Open: distal segment free; isolate or bias a muscle/joint angle
Closed: distal segment fixed; co-contraction and distributed loading
```

Choose based on tissue protection, joint shear/compression, task demand and athlete tolerance—not labels alone.

## Maitland grades

```text
I  small amplitude, beginning of range        pain modulation
II large amplitude, within available range    pain / movement tolerance
III large amplitude, into resistance          mobility
IV small amplitude, at end range               mobility
V  small, fast thrust at end range             manipulation by trained clinician
```

The grade is not the whole treatment: direction, examination findings, irritability, contraindications and reassessment decide appropriateness.

## Proprioception, plyometrics, hydrotherapy

- Proprioception: progress from stable to unstable, predictable to perturbed, two-leg to one-leg, and slow to fast tasks.
- Plyometrics: eccentric braking followed by rapid concentric action; require adequate strength, landing control and tissue readiness.
- Aquatic therapy unloads body weight and changes resistance; water depth, buoyancy, drag and temperature alter the dose.

## Easy ways to remember it

- Progress in this order: **restore movement, restore activation, build strength, then practise the sport task**.
- Maitland grades I and II are usually used for gentle pain or movement tolerance; grades III and IV emphasise mobility; grade V is a thrust performed by an appropriately trained clinician.
- Increase difficulty step by step: **range, load, speed, complexity, then unpredictable sport demands**.

# B — Assessment, Diagnosis & Clinical Reasoning

## Examination sequence

Examine the athlete in this order:

1. Take the subjective history and screen for red flags.
2. Observe the athlete and assess a relevant functional task.
3. Palpate, measure active and passive range of motion, and test resisted movement.
4. Perform manual muscle, neurological and circulation checks.
5. Add special tests and a validated outcome measure.
6. Form a working diagnosis and differential diagnosis.
7. Make a plan and reassess the response.

### Four-part clinical note

**Subjective:** history, symptoms, mechanism, goals, 24-hour response.
**Objective:** measured findings and test conditions.
**Assessment:** interpretation, differential, irritability, impairments and functional meaning.
**Plan:** intervention, education, load, referral/escalation and review date.

## Diagnostic accuracy

```text
Sensitivity = true positives/(true positives + false negatives)
Specificity = true negatives/(true negatives + false positives)
Positive likelihood ratio = sensitivity/(1−specificity)
Negative likelihood ratio = (1−sensitivity)/specificity
```

Use a high-sensitivity test to help rule out when negative and a high-specificity test to help rule in when positive. Test accuracy varies by examiner, population, disease stage and reference standard.

## Common exam associations (not stand-alone diagnoses)

| Test | Common association |
| --- | --- |
| Lachman | Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) integrity; often taught as a sensitive ACL test |
| Pivot shift | Rotational ACL instability; often taught as a more specific ACL test |
| McMurray/Thessaly | Meniscal involvement; interpret with history and other findings |
| Anterior ankle drawer | Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL)/lateral ankle instability |
| Talar tilt | Calcaneofibular ligament (CFL)/lateral ligament stress |
| Thompson/Simmonds | Achilles tendon rupture screen |
| Apprehension/relocation | Anterior shoulder instability |
| Neer/Hawkins-Kennedy | Shoulder pain/impingement provocation; not a single-tissue diagnosis |
| Speed/Yergason | Biceps-related provocation patterns |
| Flexion–abduction–external rotation (FABER) | Hip/sacroiliac-region provocation; location of symptoms matters |
| Straight-leg raise/slump | Neural mechanosensitivity/radicular pattern; structural differentiation matters |

## Manual muscle testing and range of motion

```text
0 no visible/palpable contraction
1 trace contraction
2 full range in gravity-eliminated position
3 full range against gravity
4 moderate resistance
5 normal/maximal resistance for the test position
```

End-feel: hard (bone), firm (capsule/muscle), soft (tissue approximation), empty (pain stops movement), or abnormal/springy depending on context. Document pain, quality, range, side and position.

## Outcome measures

| Domain | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Pain | Numeric Pain Rating Scale / Visual Analogue Scale |
| Lower limb function | Lower Extremity Functional Scale; Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score; International Knee Documentation Committee score |
| Upper limb | Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand / QuickDASH |
| Tendon | Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment–Achilles / –Patellar |
| Psychological readiness | Anterior Cruciate Ligament–Return to Sport after Injury scale and similar sport-specific tools |

## Red flags and referral

Progressive neurological deficit, fracture/dislocation concern, vascular compromise, infection/systemic illness, unexplained night pain/weight loss, cauda-equina features, severe mechanism or deteriorating consciousness override routine exercise testing.

## Easy ways to remember it

- Examine in this order: **history, observation, function, nerves and circulation, then special tests**.
- **Rule out danger before naming the injured tissue.**
- For every test ask three simple questions: **What structure or movement is stressed? What is the best comparison or reference? How reliable is this test in this situation?**

# B — Sports Physiotherapy, Injury Management & Return to Play

## Acute soft-tissue framework

Early care focuses on **protection, elevation, avoiding unnecessary
anti-inflammatory use, compression and education**. Later care focuses on
**progressive loading, a constructive recovery outlook, improving blood flow
and exercise**.

The key idea is not to immobilise everything or to eliminate all symptoms; protect serious tissue, control unnecessary aggravation, educate, then progressively reload capacity.

## Injury-specific anchors

| Problem | High-yield cue |
| --- | --- |
| Lateral ankle sprain | The anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) is most commonly involved, then the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL); restore range of motion, strength, balance and sport-specific loading |
| Syndesmotic sprain | “High ankle” pattern; often slower and different from uncomplicated lateral sprain |
| Hamstring strain | Rebuild isometric strength, lengthened loading, high-speed running exposure and sport demands progressively |
| Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction | Criteria-based strength, range of motion, effusion, neuromuscular control, hopping/running and psychological readiness; time alone is insufficient |
| Groin pain | Doha taxonomy: adductor-related, iliopsoas-related, inguinal-related and pubic-related; examination is symptom-location and resisted/palpation based |
| Stress injury | Respect bone-risk site, load history and imaging/medical referral; pain suppression is not healing |
| Tendinopathy | Progressive tendon capacity and energy-storage loading; avoid promising a single universal protocol |

## Return-to-sport continuum

| Stage | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Return to participation | The athlete can take part with a modified load |
| Return to sport | The athlete can complete full practice demands |
| Return to performance | The athlete can tolerate competition-level demands |

### Exit criteria

- Symptoms/effusion controlled and stable.
- Full or functionally adequate range of motion; strength and rate-of-force/power appropriate to sport.
- Limb symmetry interpreted alongside absolute capacity and task quality; **a limb symmetry index (LSI) of at least 90% is a common screen, not a universal clearance rule**.
- Hop/change-of-direction/running tests, repeated exposure and sport-specific workload tolerated.
- Psychological readiness and athlete shared decision included.
- Medical, physiotherapy, coaching and athlete communication aligned.

## Prevention cues

- Neuromuscular warm-ups such as FIFA 11+ are programmes, not one exercise.
- Nordic hamstring exercise targets eccentric hamstring capacity; adherence and progression matter.
- Copenhagen adduction targets adductor strength; dose to capacity.
- Screening identifies modifiable risk or baseline, but a screen alone does not predict an individual injury with certainty.

## Easy ways to remember it

- Early injury care means **protect and educate**; later care means **restore load and exercise capacity**.
- Return to sport asks three plain questions: **Can the body do the task? Will the athlete tolerate the demand repeatedly? Has the athlete and team agreed that returning now is sensible?**
- Groin categories in the Doha agreement are **adductor-related, iliopsoas-related, inguinal-related and pubic-related**.

# B — Sports Emergencies, On-Field Management & Travel

## Emergency action plan

An emergency action plan should cover five tasks: **prevent problems where
possible, plan and rehearse roles/equipment/access, recognise danger, respond
with the primary survey and emergency activation, then document and improve
the plan afterwards.**

## Primary survey

The primary survey checks, in order, **catastrophic bleeding, airway with
cervical-spine awareness, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure to
the environment**. Treat life threats as found; use local emergency protocols
and qualified responders.

- Attach an **automated external defibrillator (AED)** and follow its prompts as soon as it is available for a suspected cardiac arrest; minimise pauses in high-quality **cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)**.
- Severe bleeding: direct pressure/tourniquet according to training and site protocol.
- Suspected spinal injury: manual stabilisation, airway/breathing priorities, coordinated movement and appropriate equipment; do not delay life-saving care for an ideal transfer technique.

## Concussion / Sport Concussion Assessment Tool 6 (SCAT6)

For a suspected concussion:

1. If a red flag is present, arrange emergency medical assessment or transport.
2. If no red flag is present, remove the athlete from play and assess using more than one type of information.
3. Do not allow same-day return to play.
4. Use a symptom-limited, gradual return-to-learn and return-to-sport pathway after appropriate medical assessment.

Red flags include deteriorating consciousness, repeated vomiting, seizure, severe/worsening headache, neck pain/tenderness, double vision, focal weakness/tingling, unusual behaviour or increasing confusion. The Sport Concussion Assessment Tool 6 supports acute assessment; it is not a stand-alone diagnosis and normal findings do not erase clinical concern.

## Heat and cold emergencies

| Condition | Priority |
| --- | --- |
| Exertional heat stroke | Rapid recognition and **cold-water immersion/cooling immediately**; do not delay cooling for transport when safe protocol permits |
| Heat exhaustion | Remove from heat, cool, assess hydration/medical status; exclude heat stroke |
| Hypothermia | Remove wet exposure, gentle handling, rewarm and assess airway/circulation; severe cases need medical care |
| Altitude illness | Stop ascent; assess symptoms; descent/oxygen/medical care according to severity and protocol |

## Travel readiness

- Carry the emergency action plan, medical kit, emergency contacts, medication/therapeutic-use-exemption documents and a record of allergies/conditions with appropriate privacy controls.
- Use movement breaks during long travel; protect sleep, meal timing and hydration.
- After time-zone change, anchor local sleep/wake and light exposure; reduce high-risk intensity until alertness and recovery are acceptable.
- Infection-control, food/water safety, climate, altitude, venue access and insurance/evacuation plans belong in the travel risk assessment.

## Easy ways to remember it

- On the field, look in this order: **catastrophic bleeding, airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure**.
- Suspected exertional heat stroke: **start cooling immediately and keep reassessing**.
- Suspected concussion: **look for red flags, remove the athlete from play, then begin a gradual rehabilitation pathway**.

# B — Sports Medicine, Pharmacology, Nutrition & Research Basics

## Clinical–clean-sport rule

For a medicine decision, first diagnose the problem and consider non-drug
options. Then check the medicine’s indication, route, dose, interactions and
side effects. Check its anti-doping status and whether a therapeutic use
exemption is needed. Document the decision and reassess the athlete.

Never assume a medicine is permitted because it is common, prescribed, inhaled, topical or bought without prescription. Check the current **World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA)** List and **National Anti-Doping Agency India (NADA India)** guidance.

## High-yield pharmacology cues

- **Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)** may affect gastrointestinal, renal and cardiovascular risk; they can mask symptoms and should not replace load modification or diagnosis.
- Glucocorticoid status depends on route and competition period; consult the current List and route-specific guidance.
- Opioids and sedating medicines can impair reaction, judgement and safe participation.
- Beta-2 agonists, stimulants, anabolic agents, peptide hormones and masking agents are frequent anti-doping categories.
- Document allergies, interactions, contraindications and the athlete’s consent/understanding.

## Nutrition for performance and rehabilitation

| Need | Principle |
| --- | --- |
| Energy availability | Chronic mismatch between intake and expenditure can impair health and performance; screen for relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S) risk |
| Carbohydrate | Supports high-intensity work and glycogen restoration; timing depends on training density |
| Protein | Distribute adequate high-quality protein; needs may rise during immobilisation/rehabilitation, but prescribe individually |
| Fat | Essential for energy, membranes and hormone-related functions; do not eliminate it |
| Hydration | Match duration, climate, sweat rate and sodium losses; avoid both severe dehydration and overdrinking |
| Supplements | Food first; check evidence, contamination risk, interaction, dose and third-party testing |

Approximate energy values: carbohydrate **4 kcal/g**, protein **4 kcal/g**, fat **9 kcal/g**. Numbers such as protein g/kg/day are starting points, not universal prescriptions; consider injury, body size, total energy, renal/hepatic status and dietitian input.

## Research design ladder

One useful evidence hierarchy is:

1. Systematic review or meta-analysis.
2. Randomised controlled trial.
3. Cohort study.
4. Case-control study.
5. Cross-sectional study.
6. Case series or case report.
7. Expert opinion.

The hierarchy is a shortcut, not a verdict: question, bias, precision, applicability and study quality matter.

### Population–intervention–comparison–outcome (PICO) and statistics

```text
P = population/problem     I = intervention/exposure
C = comparator             O = outcome

Relative risk (RR) = risk exposed / risk control
Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = risk control − risk exposed
Number needed to treat (NNT) = 1 / ARR
Odds ratio (OR) = odds exposed / odds control
```

- RCTs support causal intervention inference when well designed; observational studies are often essential when randomisation is unethical/impractical.
- A p-value is not effect size; read the confidence interval and clinical threshold.
- Sensitivity catches disease; specificity clears disease. Predictive values depend on prevalence.
- Bias types: selection, measurement/information, recall, confounding, publication and attrition bias.

## Evidence-to-practice question

When applying research, ask: **Is the finding trustworthy? Is the effect
important? Does it apply to this athlete? What are the benefits, harms, cost
and athlete preferences?**

## Easy ways to remember it

- Before believing a research result, ask four questions: **Who was studied? What was done? What was it compared with? What outcome was measured?**
- Research designs move from **summarising many studies, to randomised experiments, to following groups, to looking back at records, to describing cases**.
- For medicines, check **route, competition status, side effects and documentation**.
